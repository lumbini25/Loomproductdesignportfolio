# Visual Inspiration Guide — Product Design Portfolio

## Purpose

Use this document as the visual and storytelling brief while building my portfolio with Claude Code. Treat it as design principles to explore and test, not a rigid template or a reason to copy another designer's site.

The portfolio should feel **visually striking, editorial, thoughtful, and approachable**. It should communicate strong product-design judgment through the work itself, with particular emphasis on AI-native products, coded prototypes, clear problem-solving, and evidence of impact.

## Personal positioning and tone

Position me as a multidisciplinary product designer who bridges research and synthesis, UX strategy and information architecture, UI design and visual craft, prototyping and implementation, and thoughtful use of AI.

My background in history, archives, and research informs how I approach design: investigate carefully, look for patterns, understand context, and make complex information easier to navigate. Let this influence the editorial sensibility, captions, composition, and storytelling. **Do not make the site look like a museum exhibit** or cover it with decorative historical imagery.

Voice: confident, thoughtful, approachable, specific rather than inflated. Show the reasoning behind design decisions. Distinguish verified outcomes from goals or hypotheses.

### Homepage headline

The headline should be **personal and impactful**, not a literal description of my history-and-archives background and not a generic “AI designer” tagline. The exact headline is intentionally undecided. Explore several strong options in context before choosing one. Use the short introduction beneath it to explain how my research background shapes my design approach.

Possible creative territory includes curiosity, making sense of complexity, or shaping experiences, but do not lock into a line without comparing alternatives.

## Information architecture

Use a simple, low-friction structure:
- Home
- Work (can link to the project section on the homepage)
- About
- Resume
- Contact

Keep the top navigation simple. Make resume and LinkedIn easy to access, and include a clear contact call to action in the navigation and footer.

For case studies, add in-page section links when they improve navigation, include a next-project recommendation at the end, and make it easy to return to the work overview.

## Homepage direction

### Visual impression

The landing page should be **visually striking on first view**. Explore a hybrid of immersive art direction and bold editorial composition: a strong headline, distinctive typography, a prominent project visual, and a considered layout. The exact hero composition is open for experimentation. Compare options rather than assuming the hero must use a particular animation, background, or split layout.

### Featured project and project order

The first project is **Knowunity — Active Recall**, because it demonstrates an AI-native learning experience and coded prototyping. Present projects in this order:

1. **Knowunity — Active Recall** — AI-powered, voice-based active-recall learning experience; coded prototype.
2. **WTV Consulting** — intranet information architecture and usability improvements.
3. **TDK / Trusted Positioning** — enterprise venue-management and indoor-positioning workflows.
4. **Anewvista Community Services** — accessible digital learning for older adults.

Knowunity should receive the strongest visual emphasis and be treated as the featured case study. The other projects should still have clear, distinct visual identities and concise descriptions.

### Project cards

Use large, high-quality visual thumbnails with a concise project title, short problem/product descriptor, my role or contribution where useful, a meaningful outcome when supported by evidence, and a small set of relevant skill tags when they add context.

Cards should feel visual and editorial, not like dense resume tiles. Use subtle hover feedback and clear focus states. Do not make important information available only on hover.

Use actual project assets—screenshots, prototypes, diagrams, or mockups—where available. Do not use generated placeholder images as if they were screenshots of my real work. If an asset is missing, create a clearly identified placeholder during development or ask me for the real asset.

## Color system

Use a restrained, mostly neutral portfolio shell with **project-specific color concentrated in each project's opening section**.

- **Project opening/hero:** use that project's own color as a distinctive accent in the opening visual or cover.
- **Rest of the case study:** transition to predominantly white and grey.
- Let product screenshots, diagrams, and selected evidence carry color naturally.
- Use grey for supporting surfaces, dividers, captions, and secondary details.
- Avoid decorative colors throughout the case study just to make it feel more colorful.

Each project should feel distinct at the top, while the reading experience remains calm, sophisticated, and consistent. Do not force one accent color across every project. Preserve project-brand colors when working with real assets.

## Typography

Explore a clean sans-serif as the core typeface, with a serif used **sparingly as an editorial accent**.

Try the serif in the homepage headline and selected case-study titles if it improves the composition. Keep body copy, navigation, labels, metadata, captions, technical details, and functional UI in a clean sans-serif by default.

Do not automatically use serif type for every heading or pull quote. Compare serif and sans-serif headline treatments in the actual layout and keep the serif only where it adds character without harming clarity. Prioritize readability, hierarchy, responsive behavior, comfortable line lengths, and thoughtful spacing.

## Case-study storytelling

### Core structure

Use a concise, repeatable **Problem → Solution → Impact** narrative. A reader should understand the problem, key design choices, and outcome without reading every paragraph.

A flexible outline:

1. **Opening:** project title, one-sentence summary, strong visual or prototype, and compact details such as role, timeline, collaborators, and scope when relevant. The project's signature color may be prominent here.
2. **Problem:** what was not working, for whom, why it mattered, and the research or evidence that framed the problem.
3. **Solution:** the core idea, important design decisions, my contribution, trade-offs, constraints, and visual evidence showing how the solution works.
4. **Impact:** measured outcomes where available, usability findings, validation, or what changed. Label estimates, goals, qualitative feedback, and measured results accurately.
5. **Closing visual:** a bold final mockup, key screen, or working prototype; a concise impact summary; and a next-project link.

Adapt the structure to the project. Do not add sections just to fill a template.

### Evidence and visual rhythm

Interweave concise explanations with relevant evidence:
- Product screenshots and prototypes
- Annotated screens
- Process photos, where useful
- Diagrams or workflow maps
- Decision matrices, when they clarify trade-offs
- Usability findings and genuine user feedback
- Before-and-after comparisons, when available

Every visual should explain a decision, demonstrate the product, or support a claim. Add captions that tell readers what to notice. Avoid long stretches of text and galleries with no explanation.

Keep design-system documentation minimal unless the design system itself is central to the project.

### Credibility

- Separate my contribution from collaborators' work.
- Do not invent metrics, quotes, user feedback, or business results.
- If a project was a concept, prototype, or paused before launch, say so clearly.
- Present qualitative results as qualitative, not quantitative.
- Show the reasoning behind important decisions and trade-offs.

## Motion and interaction

Use subtle, purposeful motion: gentle project-card hover transitions, restrained transitions between sections or states, and small interaction cues that make the interface feel responsive.

Prioritize **working project demos, real navigation, and useful interactions** over decorative animation.
- Keep decorative elements static unless movement serves a clear purpose.
- Avoid motion that delays content or makes the site feel heavy.
- Respect reduced-motion preferences.
- Ensure interactive elements work with keyboard and touch input.
- Do not create fake buttons, dead chips, nonfunctional demo controls, or links that appear active but do nothing.
- If a demo is not implemented, label it honestly.

## Responsive behavior and accessibility

Design mobile layouts intentionally rather than simply shrinking desktop:
- Simplify large visuals and shorten case-study content for quick scanning.
- Preserve the Problem → Solution → Impact story.
- Keep titles, captions, and controls readable.
- Avoid horizontal overflow and tiny tap targets.
- Keep essential content visible without hover.
- Maintain sufficient contrast and visible keyboard focus.
- Use semantic heading order and accessible labels.
- Respect reduced-motion preferences.
- Optimize images and avoid unnecessary heavy effects.

## Implementation guardrails for Claude Code

1. **Inspect before changing.** Review the existing codebase, routes, components, styling system, and available project assets before making structural changes.
2. **Preserve established design decisions.** Reuse existing typography, color variables, spacing conventions, and components when they fit. Do not casually replace working systems to match a reference.
3. **Build real behavior.** Navigation, buttons, project links, in-page anchors, and demos must work as presented.
4. **Use real content and assets.** Do not fabricate screenshots, testimonials, project outcomes, or metrics.
5. **Avoid overdesign.** Do not add gradients, glass effects, decorative illustrations, excessive borders, or motion unless they clearly support the art direction.
6. **Keep components consistent.** Reuse navigation, project cards, captions, metadata, and case-study sections where appropriate.
7. **Validate responsive layouts.** Check desktop and mobile, keyboard interaction, focus states, reduced motion, and overflow.
8. **Keep performance in mind.** Use appropriately sized images, avoid unnecessary client-side code, and keep motion lightweight.
9. **Test after implementation.** Verify routes, anchors, interactions, and supported build/lint checks. Fix broken states instead of leaving visible placeholders.
10. **Show options before locking uncertain choices.** For the homepage headline, serif usage, and hero composition, create a small number of contrasting options and let me compare them before committing.

## Decisions intentionally left open

Do not treat these as settled:
- The exact homepage headline.
- The final hero composition and whether it uses atmospheric imagery, oversized typography, or a hybrid.
- The specific serif font and exactly where it appears.
- The final accent colors for individual projects, until real assets and existing styles are inspected.

Explore these visually within the overall direction rather than asking for every minor preference up front.

## Final design principle

**Make the first impression memorable; make the work easy to understand.**

The homepage can be expressive and personality-led. Case studies should be concise, evidence-driven, and predominantly white and grey after their project-specific opening sections. Use editorial typography and thoughtful storytelling to let my research background influence the portfolio without turning it into a museum-themed website.
