# Lumbini voice: case study copy

Write case study text the way Lumbini writes when she is being plain and evidence-first. The tone is Option A, Curious editor, but in case studies it shows up as precision and visible reasoning, never as flourish.

## Scope

Covers: at-a-glance block, Problem, Solution, Impact, captions, section headings, card titles on the work page.
Does not cover: About, hero line, beliefs cards. Those have a warmer register and one allowed dry aside. Do not carry that humor into case studies.

## Locked decisions

- US English spelling.
- No em dashes anywhere. Use a period, comma, colon or parentheses. Ranges use "to" ("January to May 2024").
- Sentences under 25 words. One idea per sentence.
- Paragraphs of 1 to 3 sentences. Never more than 4.
- "I" for her work, "we" for the team, every time. Name who did what when it is not her.
- **Active voice only.** No passive constructions (a form of "be" plus a past participle). Write "I led the project," never "The project was led." The subject does the action.
- **Never frame her as a pixel pusher.** Her work is decisions, structure and ownership, not surface polish.
- **Never state a seniority level, anywhere.** No seniority label of any kind (not "junior," "senior," "associate," and not the level wording on the old Framer site's metadata). No years of experience. Describe the work and the ownership instead.
- **The word "accessible" is allowed.** It can mean reaching a feature on a device or it can mean WCAG. In the same sentence, say which one. Example: "Chat was not accessible on the watch (this is about reaching a feature, not WCAG)."
- Tone A sets the sentences. It does not soften evidence. At-a-glance, numbers, sample sizes, ship status and outcome lines stay plain and numbers-first.

## Active voice rules

- The test: if the sentence is a form of "be" plus a past participle (was led, were tested, is built), rewrite it.
- Be plus an adjective is not passive and is fine: "The flow is confusing."
- When the actor is unknown, name the closest true actor: the stakeholder, participants, my teammate, the team. If none is true, restructure with an intransitive verb: "Completion rose." "The design did not ship."
- Never hide her own action to avoid saying "I." Say "I."
- Never take credit for a teammate's action. Active voice makes this easy: "My teammate designed the Maze test. I analyzed the results."

Rewrites:

| Passive | Active |
| --- | --- |
| The project was led by me | I led the project |
| Users were interviewed | I interviewed users |
| The design was not shipped | The design did not ship |
| Findings were synthesized into themes | I grouped the findings into themes |
| The prototype was built in code | I built the prototype in code |
| Accessibility was prioritized | I set WCAG 2.1 AA as the baseline |
| A 100% increase was reported | The stakeholder reported a 100% increase |

## Role, team and anti-pixel-pusher rules

- Role and Team are separate fields. Role is her function title for that project. Team states ownership.
- Confirmed: Bloom and Heal is Product Designer, sole designer. WTV is Product Designer, one of two designers. Knowunity is UX designer. TDK is sole designer, but the discipline title is unresolved, so write [NEEDS: TDK role title]. For any other project, write [NEEDS: role] instead of guessing.
- Do not use: pixel pusher, pixel perfect as a boast, "made it look good," "polished the UI," "mockups" as the main deliverable.
- Do not use a bare "UI Designer" title. This is a judgment call, not a stated rule. If she wants it back, remove this line.
- Describe work by the decision and who it served: structure, flow, constraint, research, handoff. Visual work is described by the problem it solved, not by how it looks.

## How she writes (from her own samples)

1. Lead with the user and the constraint, then the decision.
2. Source and sample sit in the same sentence as the number.
3. She says who reported a result when she did not measure it.
4. She lists concrete examples in one sentence instead of using an abstraction.
5. Titles say what changed for the user, in plain words.
6. Verbs are plain: found, cut, moved, tested, flattened, mapped, built.

## Informal register: what her unpolished writing shows

Source: one short unedited description of Bloom and Heal (about 60 words). Treat these as observations from a small sample.

- **Status and context come first.** She opens with what the work was (a bootcamp project she completed) before describing the product. Keep this order. Ship status and project type belong in the first sentence of a case study.
- **She explains a product as a chain.** What it tracks, what it does when that happens, what the user already set up. Sequence carries the explanation, not adjectives. Use this for any "how it works" passage.
- **Her clauses are short. Her sentences are long because she joins them.** She chains clauses with a dash (typed as a hyphen). Her clauses run roughly 11 to 25 words. Splitting at each dash gives sentences that already fit the 25-word cap, so the cap should not flatten her voice.
- **She uses her own plain nouns.** "Quick reset task," "coping mechanism," "onboarding step." Keep her nouns. Do not swap in jargon.
- **She states what and how. She does not state why.** When the source material does not give the reason for a decision, write [NEEDS: reason for X] and ask her. Never invent it. Check the project's facts file first, because the reason is often already on her live page.
- **Her modal verb is honest for a concept.** "The app will track..." reads as a design, not a shipped product. For concept and course projects, keep the subject as the design or the app, and use "would." Never write in a way that implies it launched.
- **Do not copy her slips.** Lowercase "iwatch" and "iphone" become Apple Watch and iPhone. Fix tense slips. Fix capitalization of project names.
- **Gloss jargon once.** Expand HRV to heart rate variability on first use in a case study, then use HRV.

## Model sentences

Copy the pattern, not the content. Numbers here are her Anewvista facts and are not for reuse elsewhere.

- Card title pattern: "Turned a chaotic multi-tool interface into a single guided flow, sales reps could finish without training"
- Evidence with source and sample: "I had access to two primary data sources: an accessibility survey (n=19) and five months of Google Analytics data (January to May 2024)."
- Attributed result: "The stakeholder informed that they have seen a 100% increase in their grantors for the year 2024."
- Plain explainer pattern (structure and punctuation demo only, verify facts against the case study): "I completed Bloom and Heal as part of the Designlab UX Academy bootcamp. It is a wellness app for Apple Watch and iPhone. The app would track a drop in heart rate variability (HRV) and notify the user. The user can then do a quick reset task. They define their own coping mechanism during onboarding."

## Case study shape

Problem, then Solution, then Impact. A reader should get all three from headings and the first sentence of each section.

**At-a-glance block** (first thing under the title, stacks at 390px): project type, role, team, timeline, platform, main constraint, ship status, evidence, result with its qualifier. Every field is a plain fact. If a field is unknown, write [NEEDS: field] and do not fill it.

**Headings state the finding or decision**, not the topic. Good: "User interviews informed minimum touch target and font scale decisions." Bad: "Research," or anything with literary flourish.

**Patterns worth using (structure only, never wording from other designers' sites):**
- A heading that states the finding, followed by one to three sentences of cause.
- One short statement line near the top that holds a single sourced number.
- An outcomes block that says plainly what is and is not claimed.
- Captions that tell the reader what to notice in the visual.

**Questions as headings** are allowed when a finding follows directly. She does this in her research writing: pose the question, then answer it.

**Three-beat insight:** finding, then cause, then confirmation. Rewrite in plain words, one sentence per beat.

## Calibration

- Hedge where evidence is thin, commit where it is strong.
- Small samples carry their label beside the number: "6 users, 2 tasks."
- Use one name for each test across the whole case study, with its n. Example: "concept test (n=5)." Do not alternate between concept test, mid-fidelity test and usability test for the same session.
- A target is a target. A projection is a projection. A stakeholder report is a stakeholder report. A school or course project says so in the same sentence as the claim.
- If a design did not ship, say so near the top in one plain line, in active voice: "This design did not ship."
- If there is no outcome data, say "no outcome data." Do not imply results.
- Do not generalize a finding past its count. If one participant did the opposite, "participants expected X" is wrong. Use the count or write "most."
- Negative results and trade-offs are stated plainly.

## Project rules

- **Knowunity:** frame as a course project built on a real business problem. Adoption by the company is unknown. The 25% activation and 70% completion figures are design targets, not results. Name the tool once, as a fact, in active voice (for example, "I built the coded prototype with Claude Code."). The body talks about her decisions in first person. Do not narrate AI as a teammate.
- **WTV:** the design did not ship. Team of two designers. My teammate designed the Maze test. I analyzed the results. Use 6 users, 2 tasks beside the 100% completion figure.
- **Anewvista:** headline result is stakeholder-reported, never in the title as fact. Write "The stakeholder reported..."
- **TDK:** label each outcome figure measured or projected. Do not put projections in the results headline.
- **Bloom and Heal:** course project at Designlab UX Academy, sole designer, Apple Watch and iPhone. Say health and wellness. Do not claim healthtech. Keep the design-as-subject rule: the app would, not the app does. The concept test had 5 participants. Counts beyond that come from the project's facts file, not from memory.
- If metrics-bank.md, a project's facts.md or the project files give a different fact, they win. Every number comes from there or from Lumbini, never from memory or from this skill.

## Privacy

Research files contain participants' personal, health and identity details. Never quote or paraphrase anything that identifies a participant. Say "a participant." Do not publish names, transcripts or test summaries.

## Do not write

- Passive voice. Pixel-pusher framing. Any seniority label or years-of-experience claim.
- Generic phrases: "user-centered," "powerful," "seamless," "leverage," "empower," "passionate," "journey," "solutions" as a noun for her work.
- Self-grading lessons ("I improved my visual design"). A lesson names one thing learned from one finding.
- Unattributed results in titles.
- Stacked connectives: furthermore, moreover, hence, thus, consequently, nevertheless. Let sentence order carry the logic.
- Long paragraphs of narrated evidence.
- Template leftovers: bracketed placeholders, template credits, cut-off words.
- Anything copied from the Bobby Shaw or Carmen Rincon pages.
- Invented metrics, quotes, user feedback or reasons. Use [NEEDS: ...] instead.

## Process

1. Read the project's source material, its facts file and metrics-bank.md if present.
2. Draft the at-a-glance block first. Mark gaps with [NEEDS: ...].
3. Draft Problem, Solution, Impact in that order.
4. For every design decision, check that a reason is in the source. If not, flag it.
5. Write headings last, as plain statements of the finding or decision.
6. Write the card title last, outcome-led, using the TDK title pattern.
7. Run the checklist.
